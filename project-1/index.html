CS180 / Project 1

Colourizing Images
of the Russian Empire

Overview

The problem

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire using three exposures, each captured through a different colour filter: blue, red, and green. To reconstruct a single colour image, the three channels must be translated relative to one another, correcting for small differences in camera position. The goal of this project is to implement an alignment algorithm and apply it to a set of example images provided by CS180!!

Method

How the alignment works

At a high level, the process can be broken down into four stages: separate the glass-plate scan into its three exposures, choose a way to score how well two channels match, search for the translation with the best score, and finally stack the channels on top of each other based on their optimal translations. Blue stays fixed as the reference channel, while green and red are shifted independently to line up with blue.

  1. Split the original scan into blue, green, and red channels.
  2. Crop distracting borders from the images used for scoring.
  3. Search over possible x and y shifts using some distance metric (L2 or NCC).
  4. Apply the winning shifts to the full channels and combine them.

Separating the three exposures

Each input file is one tall grayscale image containing the blue, green, and red exposures stacked vertically. I divide its height by three and slice out three equal-sized images: blue from the top, green from the middle, and red from the bottom. Keeping the slices the same size is also important for comparing their pixels without shape or broadcasting errors.

Comparing two alignments

I implemented two simple scoring functions and pass either one into the same alignment search. Both scores are evaluated for every candidate displacement, and the displacement with the lowest loss is retained.

L2: L2 sums the squared difference between corresponding pixels. Perfectly matching pixels contribute zero to the loss, while large pixel intensity differences receive a much larger penalty as they are squared.

NCC: normalized cross-correlation first subtracts the mean from each channel, then measures the similarity between the two resulting pixel patterns. This makes the comparison less dependent on their overall brightness. Higher correlation normally means a better match; my function returns the negative of NCC so that the alignment code can minimize NCC in exactly the same way as L2.

Scoring only the overlapping pixels

Translating one channel, over another, creates areas where the two images no longer overlap. Wrapping pixels around to the opposite side would introduce false image content (np.roll), while padding the empty area with white or black would distort the loss. That is why for every candidate horizontal and vertical shift I calculated the rectangular region shared by both channels and evaluated the loss only on those overlapping pixels.

For example, when the moving channel is shifted to the right, pixels along its new left edge have no overlap with the reference image. The corresponding non-overlapping strip is removed from both arrays before the loss is calculated.

Single scale

Exhaustive search

The first version uses a straightforward exhaustive search. Starting with an infinite best loss, my simple_align function uses two nested loops to test every horizontal and vertical displacement within a −15 to +15 pixel search range. This gives 31 × 31, or 961, candidate positions for each moving channel. At every position it finds the valid overlap, computes the selected loss function, and records the displacement whenever that loss is lower than the best one seen so far.

This naive, single-scale approach works well for the smaller JPEG files because their required displacements generally fall within the ±15-pixel search range, while exhaustive search remains computationally manageable at their lower resolution. However, some of the first few outputs were still visibly misaligned. As shown below we can see that when we look closely there is a clear green outline on the outside of the cathedral and over the trees.

Border cropping

Some early results, such as above, aligned badly because the scanned plates contain thick black and white borders that differ between channels. Those saturated edges do not describe the scene, but they contain extreme pixel differences. Since L2 squares every difference, the borders can dominate the score and pull the chosen offset away from the true alignment.

I first removed a fixed 50-pixel border from the smaller JPEGs. That worked at their scale, but the same number was too small for the much larger TIFF scans, whose borders occupy many more pixels. Hence, the final implementation trims 10% from the top, bottom, left, and right of every channel before searching. The scoring crop therefore scales with the image dimensions and works consistently for both formats.

Final note on aligning and rendering

So at this stage we were getting a reasonably decent optimal x and y offset and all that was left was aligning and rendering the images. The final translation is applied to the original, full-sized channel. In the align_and_render function, I use the winning x and y offsets to build a translation matrix, shift the channel with warpAffine function from openCV and then stack the unchanged blue channel with the aligned green and red channels.

I tried two alignment strategies. In the first, I aligned green to blue, averaged those two aligned images by dividing their sum by two so the result remained a single intensity channel, and aligned red against that combined blue-green reference. The result was reasonable, but it was not meaningfully better. Simply an experimental attempt to see if this does anything with great change.

I therefore returned to the original approach which aligns green directly to blue, align red directly to blue, and then combine all three. This is simpler and makes both displacement estimates relative to the same fixed reference.

Multi scale

Image pyramid alignment

A small search window is not enough for the high-resolution TIFF scans, where the correct displacement may be many pixels away. Expanding an exhaustive search at full resolution would make the program unnecessarily slow: increasing the radius expands the number of candidate positions in both x and y, and every candidate requires a comparison over a large image. That is why for this assignment we will be implementing a coarse-to-fine image pyramid.

I build separate pyramids for the fixed reference and the moving channel. Level 0 contains the original image, level 1 is half-sized, level 2 is half-sized again, and so on until the low resolution dimension is at most 256 pixels. The last element in each pyramid is therefore the smallest and fastest one to search.

  1. Run the complete ±15-pixel search at the coarsest pyramid level.
  2. Move one level toward the original resolution and double the x and y shifts found, because a one-pixel shift at the smaller level represents two pixels at the next level.
  3. Refine that prediction by searching only within ±3 pixels of it at the next level.
  4. Repeat the doubling and local refinement until level 0 is reached.

In this way, the coarse level handles large motion cheaply, while the higher-resolution levels only make small corrections. The final shift can be much larger than 15 pixels without ever requiring a huge full-resolution exhaustive search.

Downsampling

My first implementation followed the lecture approach: I applied a Gaussian blur to reduce high-frequency detail, then only kept every second row and column. Blurring before subsampling helps prevent fine detail from turning into false patterns at the lower resolution.

While debugging the larger images, I suspected that this custom downsampling step that I am writing might be causing the poor alignments, so I replaced it with scikit-image's anti-aliased rescale function as was provided in the starter code. It turned out that downsampling was not the underlying problem; the real fix was changing the fixed border crop into a percentage of the image size. I kept the library rescaling in the final implementation, but the experiment helped isolate the actual source of the error.

A special case

Gradient NCC for the Emir of Bukhara

The Emir image is a difficult case because the three glass-plate exposures were captured through different colour filters. A region that is bright in the blue-filtered plate may be dark in the red-filtered plate, and this change is not consistent across every material in the scene. For example, the turban, robe, wood doors, skin, and shadows all react differently to each filter. Raw NCC removes an overlap's average brightness and contrast, but it still expects matching regions to have a similar overall brightness pattern. For Emir, that assumption can cause raw NCC to choose the wrong displacement.

Instead of comparing raw brightness, I compare image gradients: locations where brightness changes sharply. I calculate these gradients with Sobel filters, which are small 3 × 3 filters applied across the image. One Sobel filter measures left-to-right brightness change, while the other measures top-to-bottom brightness change. Their outputs are called the horizontal gradient (Gx) and vertical gradient (Gy).

Flat areas such as a clear wall or sky have gradients close to zero. Strong boundaries such as the outline of the turban, face, robe seams, door frames, and other scene edges have large gradients. I combine the horizontal and vertical values into one edge-strength image using sqrt(Gx² + Gy²), called the gradient magnitude. This also treats both dark-to-light and light-to-dark transitions as strong edges, which is useful when a colour filter reverses the apparent contrast of a boundary.

Gradient-NCC alignment steps

  1. For each candidate horizontal and vertical shift, I first extract the valid overlapping region between the fixed blue channel and the moving green or red channel.
  2. I apply horizontal and vertical Sobel filters to both overlapping regions.
  3. I combine each pair of Sobel responses into a gradient-magnitude image, where stronger scene boundaries have larger values.
  4. I run NCC on these gradient images rather than on the raw pixel intensities. My implementation returns negative NCC so the existing search code can still select the lowest loss.
  5. After finding the best shift, I apply it to the original full-resolution colour channel and stack the original blue, aligned green, and aligned red channels to produce the final image.

This works especially well for Emir because the brightness of a material may change across filters, but the physical locations of its boundaries do not. Gradient NCC therefore aligns the stable geometry of the scene rather than its filter-dependent raw brightness.
